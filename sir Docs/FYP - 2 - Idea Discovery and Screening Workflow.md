## **FYP Idea Discovery and Screening Workflow** 

Repeatable weekly workflow for 3-student FYP groups 

This document contains the full operational process students should follow repeatedly across weekly meetings. It covers individual ideation, internal criticism, deeper screening, overlap checking, proposal-defense preparation, scope shaping, feasibility, and meeting readiness. 

## **1. Because your FYP group has 3 students, the workflow must begin individually** 

Your group should **not** jump immediately into one collective brainstorm. 

Instead, the correct process is: 

1. **Each student works individually first** 

2. **Then the group critiques ideas internally** 

3. **Then the group deeply analyzes shortlisted ideas** 

4. **Only then should the group come to the supervisor** 

This is important because early group brainstorming often causes: 

- premature convergence, 

- weak diversity of ideas, 

- over-attachment to the first attractive concept, 

- and less serious exploration. 

## **2. Full workflow overview** 

Your idea-selection workflow should follow this sequence: 

**Individual problem-space exploration -> individual idea generation -> individual selfcritique -> group pooling -> internal rejection of weak ideas -> solution-landscape mapping -> contribution hunting -> novelty stress testing -> prior-FYP overlap checking -> external comparison and gap analysis -> contribution statement writing -> proposaldefense simulation -> scope shaping -> feasibility planning -> final scoring -> supervisor meeting** 

Every major part of that sequence matters. 

## **3. Phase 1: Individual ideation** 

## **Each student works independently first** 

Each student should independently develop multiple candidate ideas before group discussion. 

## **Expected minimum** 

Each student should ideally bring **3-5 serious candidate ideas** . 

For a 3-student group, this gives roughly **9-15 raw ideas** at the start. 

Do not come with only one idea per student. That usually leads to weak exploration. 

## **4. Phase 1, Step A: Explore multiple problem spaces** 

Before generating project ideas, each student should first identify several problem spaces. For each problem space, ask: 

- Who has the problem? 

- What is the current workflow? 

- Where does it fail? 

- Why does that failure matter? 

- What do people currently do instead? 

- Where might a real technical or system-level contribution exist? 

At this point, do not ask: “What app can we build?” 

Ask: “Where is there room for a real contribution?” 

## **5. Phase 1, Step B: Map existing solution categories** 

Before claiming that there is a gap, you must understand what already exists. For each promising problem space, scan: 

- commercial products, 

- open-source tools, 

- research prototypes or papers, 

- manual/non-digital workflows, 

- and previous departmental FYPs. 

This prevents shallow claims like: 

- there is no solution, 

- nobody has done this, 

- or this is unique. 

Many student ideas look novel only because students have not explored enough. 

## **6. Phase 1, Step C: Generate candidate ideas under FYP constraints** 

Only after exploring the problem space should each student generate candidate ideas. 

Each candidate idea should already satisfy these conditions: 

- suitable for a **two-semester FYP** , 

- substantial enough for **3 students** , 

- defensible in **Proposal Defense** , 

- capable of a meaningful **proof of concept by December** , 

- not just a CRUD system, 

- not just a generic chatbot, 

- not just a thin wrapper over existing APIs, 

- not just feature-bundling, 

- not just a recycled version of a previous FYP. 

## **7. Phase 1, Step D: Individual self-critique** 

Before bringing ideas to the group, each student should critically challenge their own ideas. 

For each idea, ask: 

- Is this actually novel? 

- Is the contribution clear? 

- Is this just integration? 

- Is this too small for 3 students? 

- Is this too broad for the available time? 

- Is it too dependent on unavailable data? 

- Is it too dependent on fragile external tools or services? 

- Is it too similar to previously completed FYPs? 

- Is the idea mainly a bundle of features rather than a contribution? 

If an idea already feels weak at this stage, do not defend it emotionally. Mark it as weak. 

## **8. What each student should bring for each initial idea** 

For every candidate idea, each student should prepare: 

- tentative title, 

- target problem, 

- target users, 

- current workflow or current way the problem is handled, 

- existing solutions, 

- possible gap, 

- possible contribution, 

- likely technical direction, 

- similarity concern with prior FYPs, 

- rough scope judgment, 

- rough feasibility risks. 

That is the minimum level of seriousness expected before group discussion. 

## **9. Phase 2: Internal group criticism and filtering** 

## **The group becomes the first review committee** 

Once each student has independently prepared ideas, the group should pool them and critically analyze them together. 

The goal is **not** to merge all ideas. 

The goal is to **reject weak ideas early** . 

## **10. Pool all raw ideas together** 

At this stage, the group should have roughly **9-15 ideas** total. 

Do not rush to choose one favorite immediately. First, compare all of them. 

## **11. Reject obviously weak ideas** 

The group should eliminate ideas that are: 

- direct copies, 

- near-copies of old FYPs, 

- too generic, 

- mostly dashboards/CRUD, 

- mostly integration, 

- dependent on AI as the only novelty claim, 

- too vague, 

- too small for 3 students, 

- too broad for the timeline, 

- too dependent on unavailable datasets, 

- too dependent on fragile external services, 

- too difficult to evaluate properly, 

- or lacking a clear contribution. 

This filtering is mandatory. Do not bring raw brainstorming to the supervisor. 

## **12. Force internal disagreement and criticism** 

A useful group rule is: 

## **Every idea should be challenged by the other two group members.** 

Ask each other: 

- What exactly is new here? 

- 

- Why is this not a copy? 

- 

- What is the actual contribution? 

- 

- Is this just feature integration? 

- 

- Is this too small? 

- 

- Is this unrealistically large? 

- Can this reach a real prototype by December? 

- Is this idea only attractive because it sounds trendy? 

Do not keep an idea just because one group member likes it. 

## **13. Keep a shortlist only** 

After internal filtering, reduce the set to roughly **3-5 serious candidate ideas** . 

Only these shortlisted ideas should move forward into deeper analysis. 

Everything else should be rejected or parked. 

**Optional but strongly useful:** Briefly note why each rejected idea was rejected. This improves seriousness and prevents the same weak idea from returning later in a disguised form. 

## **14. Phase 3: Group-level refinement and deeper analysis** 

Once weak ideas have been rejected, the group should now work together on the surviving ideas. 

This is where LLMs are especially useful. 

At this stage, LLMs should be used as: 

- brainstorming partners, 

- critics, 

- solution mappers, 

- similarity checkers, 

- gap-finders, 

- stress testers, 

- and scope shapers. 

They should **not** be used to artificially make weak ideas sound novel. 

## **15. Reframe each shortlisted idea in problem-first form** 

Each shortlisted idea should be restated clearly as: 

X users struggle with Y problem in Z context. Existing solutions do A and B, but they still fail in C and D. Our project contributes E. 

If you cannot state your idea clearly in this form, then the idea is probably still vague. 

## **16. Build a proper solution landscape map** 

For each shortlisted idea, map the solution landscape under these categories: 

- commercial products, 

- open-source tools, 

- research approaches, 

- manual/non-digital practice, 

- previous departmental FYPs. 

For each category, identify: 

- what already exists, 

- what it does well, 

- what it does not do well, 

- and where limitations repeatedly appear. 

This is stronger than writing a shallow competitor list. 

## **17. Hunt explicitly for contribution opportunities** 

Do not only ask: “Is this novel?” Also ask: “Where exactly could the real contribution come from?” 

Possible contribution types include: 

- technical improvement, 

- workflow redesign, 

- decision-support enhancement, 

- human-in-the-loop system design, 

- uncertainty-aware outputs, 

- domain-specific optimization, 

- targeting a neglected subproblem, 

- meaningful multimodal fusion, 

- stronger evaluation frameworks, 

- stakeholder-specific improvements, 

- or a non-obvious but defensible system combination. 

This must be a deliberate step. Many ideas fail because students never identify the actual contribution point. 

## **18. Run the first novelty stress test** 

Now stop being creative and start being critical. 

For each shortlisted idea, ask: 

- Is this really new? 

- Is the newness only branding? 

- Is this mostly integration? 

- Is this an old idea with a new label? 

- Is the contribution precise enough to defend? 

- Would an evaluator immediately ask, “What is new here?” 

Any idea with weak novelty should be downgraded, reshaped, or removed. 

## **19. Mandatory prior-FYP overlap check** 

This is a dedicated stage and must not be skipped. 

You must compare shortlisted ideas against the list of previously completed departmental FYPs. 

## **Important rule** 

## **Do not compare only by titles.** 

Compare each idea with prior FYPs using: 

- problem being solved, 

- target users, 

- core contribution, 

- technical pipeline, 

- input/data type, 

- output type, 

- interaction model, 

- and evaluation story. 

## **20. Classify each idea against prior FYPs** 

Each shortlisted idea should be classified as: 

- **Safe** 

- **Risky** 

- **Too Similar** 

An idea is risky if it overlaps strongly in: 

- the same core problem, 

- the same user group, 

- the same workflow, 

- the same technical pipeline, 

- the same main contribution claim, 

- the same value proposition, 

- or only differs cosmetically. 

## **21. What usually does not make an idea sufficiently different from an old FYP** 

These usually do **not** rescue a repetitive idea: 

- changing domain wording, 

- adding voice, 

- adding RAG, 

- adding a chatbot, 

- changing web to mobile, 

- changing language support, 

- switching models, 

- calling it AI-powered or agentic, 

- or polishing the interface. 

If the core problem and contribution remain the same, the idea is still risky. 

## **22. Compare against the outside world too** 

Even if an idea is sufficiently different from previous departmental FYPs, it may still be weak if it is already a common existing product or obvious solution. 

So for each shortlisted idea, compare against: 

- real products, 

- software systems, 

- open-source tools, 

- research solutions, 

- and other obvious existing approaches. 

For each one, answer: 

- What already exists? 

- What does it already do well? 

- Where does it still fall short? 

- What gap remains? 

- Is our project a true contribution, or just a local implementation of an existing concept? 

This comparison is a key part of novelty evidence. 

## **23. Write a one-sentence contribution statement** 

Every surviving idea must be reduced to one clear sentence beginning with: 

## **Our contribution is...** 

If that sentence becomes vague and depends on words like smart, innovative, intelligent, AIpowered, efficient, user-friendly, or seamless without clearly stating the new contribution, then the contribution is still unclear. 

This is one of the strongest tests of idea maturity. 

## **24. Simulate Proposal Defense objections** 

Before meeting the supervisor, each shortlisted idea should be attacked using evaluator-style questions. 

Ask: 

- What is actually new here? 

- Why is this not just integration? 

- Why is this not a copy of existing work? 

- Why is this not too similar to prior FYPs? 

- Why is this a Final Year Project rather than a semester project? 

- Why is this still feasible? 

- What is the biggest technical risk? 

- What exactly will be built by December? 

- On what grounds could this proposal be rejected? 

If your idea cannot survive this stage, it is not ready. 

## **25. Scope calibration for 3-student groups** 

Because your group has **3 students** , your idea must not feel like a one-person mini-project. 

At the same time, it must not become an overgrown product wish-list. 

You must explicitly test both sides: 

- Why is this **too small** for 3 capable students? 

- Why is this **too large** for the available time? 

## **26. Shape scope through pruning as well as expansion** 

Do not keep adding features to make the project look big. 

Instead, divide the project into: 

- **core contribution** 

- **supporting functionality** 

- **optional polish** 

- **features to remove** 

- **possible later extensions** 

This is very important. 

A strong FYP is not produced by endless feature accumulation. It is produced by a **clear contribution with proper scope** . 

## **27. What a good 3-student FYP should usually contain** 

A good team-based FYP should usually allow room for: 

- a meaningful core technical or system contribution, 

- real implementation work, 

- evaluation or validation, 

- and refinement or iteration. 

This does **not** mean you need 3 isolated modules. It means the overall project should have enough depth to justify 3 students over 2 semesters. 

## **28. Feasibility and December proof-of-concept planning** 

For each shortlisted idea, identify: 

- what absolutely must be built, 

- what counts as the minimum meaningful proof of concept by December, 

- what can be deferred, 

- key data requirements, 

- major technical risks, 

- likely blockers, 

- and fallback options. 

This prevents ideas from being accepted on aspiration alone. 

## **29. Final scoring before meeting the supervisor** 

Before coming to the supervisor, the group should score each shortlisted idea on a **1-5 scale** for: 

- novelty strength, 

- distance from prior FYPs, 

- distance from obvious existing solutions, 

- clarity of contribution, 

- technical depth, 

- suitability for 3 students, 

- fit for two semesters, 

- feasibility of December prototype, 

- and defense strength under questioning. 

Any idea weak on **novelty** or **contribution** should usually not survive, even if it sounds exciting. 

## **30. What your group should bring to the supervisor meeting** 

Your group should bring only a small number of serious candidate ideas, ideally **3-5** . 

For each shortlisted idea, be ready to present: 

1. tentative title, 

2. target problem, 

3. target users, 

4. current workflow or current context, 

5. existing solutions, 

6. closest previous departmental FYPs, 

7. why your idea is still distinct, 

8. identified gap, 

9. exact contribution statement, 

10. core technical approach, 

11. why the scope suits 3 students over 2 semesters, 

12. what could be ready by December, 

13. main risks, 

14. likely proposal-defense objections, 

15. your internal judgment of whether the idea is safe, risky, or weak. 

Do **not** come with raw brainstorming only. 

## **31. Role of the supervisor in this process** 

The supervisor meeting is **not** the first brainstorming stage. 

It is the stage where promising ideas are: 

- evaluated more rigorously, 

- checked for genuine novelty, 

- tested for real gap claims, 

- rejected if they are disguised copies, 

- reshaped if promising, 

- pruned if bloated, 

- expanded if too small, 

- and guided toward a defendable proposal. 

This means your group must do real filtering **before** the meeting. 

## **32. Proper use of LLMs** 

LLMs should be used throughout this workflow, but correctly. 

## **Use LLMs as:** 

- brainstorming partners during problem exploration, 

- critics during novelty filtering, 

- mappers during solution landscape analysis, 

- similarity checkers against prior FYPs, 

- gap-finders during comparison work, 

- stress testers during defense simulation, 

- and scope shapers during project planning. 

## **Do not use LLMs to:** 

- quickly invent one trendy idea, 

- make a weak idea sound novel, 

- hide overlap with prior FYPs, 

- avoid doing comparison work, 

- confuse feature lists with contribution, 

- replace your own critical thinking. 

LLMs are tools for thinking, not shortcuts to defensible novelty. 

## **33. This workflow is iterative** 

This workflow is meant to be repeated, not followed only once from start to finish. 

Because idea selection and refinement happen across repeated weekly meetings, groups should expect to revisit earlier stages multiple times. An idea may be generated, criticized, reshaped, compared again, narrowed, expanded, or rejected altogether. That is part of the process. 

At each cycle, the group should re-check: 

- problem clarity, 

- novelty, 

- contribution, 

- overlap with prior FYPs, 

- comparison against existing solutions, 

- scope for 3 students, 

- feasibility, 

- and likely proposal-defense objections. 

The aim of each weekly round is to bring stronger and better-defended ideas than the previous round. 

## **34. Reusable core instruction for students** 

Your job is **not** to bring one flashy idea. 

Your job is to bring a small set of serious ideas that have already survived: 

- individual exploration, 

- individual self-critique, 

- internal group criticism, 

- comparison against existing solutions, 

- comparison against prior departmental FYPs, 

- novelty stress testing, 

- contribution clarification, 

- scope calibration, 

- and proposal-defense style questioning. 

## **35. Compact one-line summary of the full process** 

**Individual problem-space exploration -> individual idea generation -> individual selfcritique -> group pooling -> internal rejection -> solution-landscape mapping -> contribution hunting -> novelty stress test -> prior-FYP overlap check -> external comparison/gap analysis -> contribution statement -> proposal-defense simulation -> scope shaping -> feasibility planning -> final scoring -> supervisor meeting** 

That is the complete workflow.
